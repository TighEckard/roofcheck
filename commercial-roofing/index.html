Commercial roofing: free inspections and quotes

For property managers, HOAs, landlords and business owners. Tell us about the building and we'll connect you with an independent commercial roofer.

Updated November 18, 2026 · By the Bedrock Roof team

Quick answer: Bedrock Roof connects owners and managers of commercial buildings, apartment and condo buildings, HOAs and churches with independent commercial roofing contractors for a free inspection and quote. Most commercial roofs are low-slope systems (TPO, EPDM, PVC, modified bitumen or built-up roofing) or metal, and many can be repaired or restored with a coating instead of fully replaced.

Who we help

  • Property managers handling one building or a portfolio.
  • HOAs and condo associations planning repairs or a reserve-funded replacement.
  • Apartment and multifamily owners.
  • Business owners: retail, restaurants, offices, medical buildings.
  • Warehouses and industrial buildings, often with metal or large single-ply roofs.
  • Churches, schools and nonprofits.

Common commercial roof types

  • TPO: a white single-ply membrane, widely used on new low-slope roofs. It reflects sunlight and has heat-welded seams.
  • EPDM: a black synthetic rubber membrane with a long track record, common in colder climates.
  • PVC: a single-ply membrane with heat-welded seams that resists chemicals and grease, which is why it's used on restaurants.
  • Modified bitumen: asphalt-based sheets applied in layers, common on smaller flat roofs.
  • Built-up roofing (BUR): the traditional "tar and gravel" roof made of alternating layers of bitumen and felt.
  • Metal: standing-seam or through-fastened panels, common on warehouses and pre-engineered buildings.
  • Roof coatings: silicone, acrylic or polyurethane coatings applied over a sound existing roof to seal it and reflect heat.

See flat roof types compared and commercial roof coatings for details.

What a commercial inspection usually covers

  1. The membrane or roof surface, seams and penetrations (HVAC curbs, pipes, vents).
  2. Drainage: drains, scuppers, gutters and any areas where water ponds.
  3. Flashing at walls, parapets and edges.
  4. Signs of trapped moisture in the insulation, sometimes checked with core cuts or an infrared or moisture survey.
  5. Rooftop equipment and foot-traffic damage.
  6. The age and warranty status of the roof.

Before you hire a commercial roofer

  • Check the contractor's state license or registration for commercial work. Rules differ by state; see our state pages.
  • Ask for certificates of general liability and workers' comp insurance, and to be named as an additional insured if your lease or lender requires it.
  • Ask whether the contractor is certified by the membrane manufacturer, which is often required for a manufacturer's system warranty.
  • Get a written scope that lists the roof system, insulation, flashing details, warranty terms and how tenants and operations will be protected during the work.
  • Ask for references from similar buildings.

Maintenance matters more on flat roofs

Flat and low-slope roofs don't shed water as quickly as steep roofs, so small problems like a clogged drain or an open seam turn into leaks faster. A twice-yearly inspection and prompt repairs are usually the cheapest way to make a commercial roof last. See our commercial roof maintenance checklist.

Frequently asked questions

Does Bedrock Roof help with commercial roofs?
Yes. Owners and managers of commercial buildings, apartment and condo buildings, HOAs, churches, warehouses and retail properties can request a free inspection and quote from an independent commercial roofing contractor.
What types of commercial roofs are there?
Most commercial buildings have low-slope or flat roofs covered with a single-ply membrane (TPO, EPDM or PVC), modified bitumen or built-up roofing. Metal roofs are common on warehouses and newer buildings. Roof coatings can extend the life of many of these systems.
How often should a commercial roof be inspected?
Many roofing manufacturers and industry groups recommend an inspection at least twice a year, typically in spring and fall, and after any major storm. Regular inspections are often a condition of keeping a manufacturer's warranty in force.
Can a commercial roof be restored instead of replaced?
Often, if the existing roof is still mostly dry and sound. A roofer can check for trapped moisture, for example with core samples or an infrared or moisture survey, and tell you whether a coating or restoration system is an option.
Do commercial roofers need a different license?
It depends on the state. Some states license commercial roofing separately from residential work, or require a general contractor license for commercial jobs. Our state pages explain each state's rules.
